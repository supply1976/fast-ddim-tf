\begin{abstract}
These notes summarize the mathematical basis of the TensorFlow DDIM implementation in this repository.
The presentation starts from the variance-preserving stochastic differential equation (VP-SDE), derives
the closed-form forward transition, derives the Gaussian posterior used by DDPM, and then rewrites the
reverse step in the DDIM parameterization used for fast sampling.  The notation is chosen to match the
implementation: $\alpha_t$ denotes the cumulative signal power, $1-\alpha_t$ denotes the noise power,
and the model may predict noise, clean image, or velocity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Denoising diffusion models learn to invert a gradual noising process.  A clean sample $\bx_0$ from the
data distribution is transformed into a noisy sample $\bx_t$ by mixing it with Gaussian noise.  The
learning problem is to train a neural network to estimate the clean signal or the injected noise from
the noisy observation.  Once trained, the model generates new samples by starting from Gaussian noise
and repeatedly applying a learned reverse transition.

This document focuses on the variance-preserving family used by DDPM and DDIM.  In this convention the
forward marginal has the form
\begin{equation}
  q(\bx_t|\bx_0) = \N\!\left(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bx_0, (1-\alpha_t)\bI\right),
  \label{eq:forward_marginal_intro}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_0=1$ and $\alpha_t$ decreases toward zero as $t$ approaches the terminal diffusion time.
Many papers denote this cumulative signal power by $\bar{\alpha}_t$; here we write $\alpha_t$ to match
the code.

\section{Notation and Time Indexing}

The random variable at diffusion time $t$ is denoted by $\bX(t)$, and a realized sample is denoted by
$\bx_t$.  For $0 \leq s < t \leq T$, $\bx_s$ is less noisy than $\bx_t$.  The implementation discretizes
time into integer indices $0,1,\ldots,N$, but the derivation is easiest in continuous time.  The two are
connected by evaluating the continuous schedule at a finite grid.

\section{Forward Process from the VP-SDE}

The variance-preserving SDE is
\begin{equation}
  d\bX(t) = -\frac{\beta(t)}{2}\bX(t)\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,d\bW(t),
  \label{eq:vp_sde}
\end{equation}
where $\beta(t)\geq 0$ is the noise-rate schedule and $\bW(t)$ is a standard Wiener process.  Informally,
$d\bW(t)$ is a Gaussian increment with covariance $dt\,\bI$, so the drift term shrinks the signal while
the diffusion term injects Gaussian noise.

For the scalar case, the transition density satisfies the Fokker--Planck equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t}q(x_t|x_s)
  =
  \frac{\beta(t)}{2}
  \left[
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x_t}\left(x_t q(x_t|x_s)\right)
    +
    \frac{\partial^2 q(x_t|x_s)}{\partial x_t^2}
  \right].
  \label{eq:fokker_planck_scalar}
\end{equation}
For vector-valued images, this equation is interpreted component-wise or, equivalently, with divergence
and Laplacian operators:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t}q(\bx_t|\bx_s)
  =
  \frac{\beta(t)}{2}
  \left[
    \nabla_{\bx_t}\!\cdot\!\left(\bx_t q(\bx_t|\bx_s)\right)
    +
    \Delta_{\bx_t}q(\bx_t|\bx_s)
  \right].
  \label{eq:fokker_planck_vector}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Closed-Form Forward Transition}

Define the integrated noise rate and cumulative signal power as
\begin{align}
  B(t) &= \int_0^t \beta(\tau)\,d\tau, \\
  \alpha_t &= \exp(-B(t)).
\end{align}
Solving the linear SDE gives the Gaussian transition
\begin{equation}
  q(\bx_t|\bx_s)
  =
  \N\!\left(
    \sqrt{\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}}\,\bx_s,
    \left(1-\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right)\bI
  \right),
  \qquad 0 \leq s < t.
  \label{eq:forward_transition}
\end{equation}
Setting $s=0$ and $\alpha_0=1$ gives the forward marginal
\begin{equation}
  q(\bx_t|\bx_0)
  =
  \N\!\left(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bx_0, (1-\alpha_t)\bI\right).
  \label{eq:forward_marginal}
\end{equation}
Therefore a noisy sample can be generated directly at any time:
\begin{equation}
  \bx_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bx_0 + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\beps,
  \qquad \beps \sim \N(\mathbf{0},\bI).
  \label{eq:q_sample}
\end{equation}
This direct formula is what makes training efficient: the model does not need to simulate every
intermediate noising step.

\subsection{Noise Schedules}

The schedule controls the signal-to-noise ratio
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}(t)=\frac{\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}.
  \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
A linear continuous schedule for $\beta(t)$ over normalized time $t\in[0,1]$ is
\begin{align}
  \beta(t) &= \beta_0 + t(\beta_1-\beta_0), \\
  \alpha_t &= \exp\!\left[-\beta_0 t - \frac{1}{2}(\beta_1-\beta_0)t^2\right].
  \label{eq:linear_schedule}
\end{align}
A simple cosine schedule can be written as
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_t = \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{t}{T}\right),
  \qquad 0\leq t\leq T.
  \label{eq:simple_cosine}
\end{equation}
The implementation also includes the improved DDPM cosine schedule, which discretizes an offset cosine
curve into per-step betas and then takes their cumulative product.

\section{Training Parameterizations}

Equation~\eqref{eq:q_sample} gives a useful identity between the clean sample, noise, and noisy sample.
The network can be trained to predict different but equivalent targets.

\subsection{Noise Prediction}

The classic DDPM target is the injected noise:
\begin{equation}
  \beps_\theta(\bx_t,t) \approx \beps.
  \label{eq:eps_prediction}
\end{equation}
Given a noise prediction, the implied clean-sample estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \bx_0^\theta(\bx_t,t)
  =
  \frac{\bx_t-\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\beps_\theta(\bx_t,t)}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}.
  \label{eq:x0_from_eps}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Clean-Image Prediction}

The model may also predict $\bx_0$ directly:
\begin{equation}
  \bx_0^\theta(\bx_t,t) \approx \bx_0.
\end{equation}
The corresponding noise estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \beps_\theta(\bx_t,t)
  =
  \frac{\bx_t-\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bx_0^\theta(\bx_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}}.
  \label{eq:eps_from_x0}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Velocity Prediction}

The implementation supports velocity prediction, a parameterization used by Salimans and Ho
for stable fast sampling and progressive distillation~\cite{salimans2022progressive}, defined by
\begin{equation}
  \bv_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\beps - \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\bx_0.
  \label{eq:velocity_target}
\end{equation}
This parameterization is numerically useful because it rotates the pair $(\bx_0,\beps)$ into coordinates
that remain well-scaled across noise levels.  If the network predicts $\bv_\theta$, the corresponding
clean and noise estimates are
\begin{align}
  \bx_0^\theta &= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bx_t - \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\bv_\theta, \\
  \beps_\theta &= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bv_\theta + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\bx_t.
  \label{eq:v_to_components}
\end{align}
These are the conversions used by \texttt{DiffusionUtility.get\_pred\_components}.

\section{Reverse Posterior for DDPM}

The true reverse conditional $q(\bx_s|\bx_t)$ depends on the unknown data distribution and is not
available in closed form.  However, the posterior conditioned on the original clean sample is available:
\begin{equation}
  q(\bx_s|\bx_t,\bx_0)
  =
  \frac{q(\bx_t|\bx_s)q(\bx_s|\bx_0)}{q(\bx_t|\bx_0)}.
  \label{eq:bayes_posterior}
\end{equation}
Because all three terms are Gaussian, this posterior is also Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
  q(\bx_s|\bx_t,\bx_0)
  =
  \N\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s,t}(\bx_t,\bx_0), \Sigma_{s,t}^2\bI\right),
  \qquad 0\leq s<t.
  \label{eq:posterior_gaussian}
\end{equation}
The mean and variance are
\begin{align}
  \boldsymbol{\mu}_{s,t}(\bx_t,\bx_0)
  &=
  \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}}\,\bx_t
  +
  \frac{1-\alpha_t/\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\sqrt{\alpha_s}\,\bx_0,
  \label{eq:posterior_mean} \\
  \Sigma_{s,t}^2
  &=
  \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}
  \left(1-\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\right).
  \label{eq:posterior_variance}
\end{align}
For adjacent discrete DDPM steps, $s=t-1$.  For accelerated sampling, $s$ can skip multiple training
indices, as long as $0\leq s<t$.

\subsection{DDPM Sampling Step}

DDPM replaces the unknown $\bx_0$ in the posterior mean with the model estimate $\bx_0^\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  \bx_s
  =
  \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}}\,\bx_t
  +
  \frac{1-\alpha_t/\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\sqrt{\alpha_s}\,\bx_0^\theta(\bx_t,t)
  +
  \Sigma_{s,t}\beps,
  \qquad \beps \sim \N(\mathbf{0},\bI).
  \label{eq:ddpm_step}
\end{equation}
This stochastic transition samples from the learned approximation to the DDPM reverse chain.

\section{DDIM Reverse Sampling}

DDIM rewrites the reverse transition so that the predicted clean image and predicted noise appear
explicitly:
\begin{equation}
  \bx_s
  =
  \sqrt{\alpha_s}\,\bx_0^\theta(\bx_t,t)
  +
  \sqrt{1-\alpha_s-\sigma_{s,t}^2}\,\beps_\theta(\bx_t,t)
  +
  \sigma_{s,t}\beps,
  \label{eq:ddim_general}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{s,t} = \eta\,\Sigma_{s,t}.
  \label{eq:ddim_sigma}
\end{equation}
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:ddim_sigma} gives the implementation form:
\begin{equation}
  \bx_s
  =
  \sqrt{\alpha_s}\,\bx_0^\theta(\bx_t,t)
  +
  \sqrt{1-\alpha_s-\eta^2\Sigma_{s,t}^2}\,\beps_\theta(\bx_t,t)
  +
  \eta\Sigma_{s,t}\beps.
  \label{eq:ddim_step}
\end{equation}
Two important cases are:
\begin{align}
  \eta &= 1, && \text{DDPM-equivalent posterior variance}, \\
  \eta &= 0, && \text{deterministic DDIM sampling}.
\end{align}
For intermediate values $0<\eta<1$, DDIM interpolates between deterministic sampling and stochastic
DDPM-like sampling.

\section{Algorithmic Summary}

Training uses random diffusion times and the closed-form noising equation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sample clean data $\bx_0$ from the dataset.
  \item Sample a timestep $t$ and Gaussian noise $\beps$.
  \item Form $\bx_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\bx_0+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\beps$.
  \item Train the network to predict the chosen target: $\beps$, $\bx_0$, or $\bv_t$.
\end{enumerate}

Sampling starts from Gaussian noise and applies Equation~\eqref{eq:ddim_step}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sample $\bx_T\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\bI)$.
  \item Choose a descending sequence $T=t_K>t_{K-1}>\cdots>t_0=0$.
  \item At each step, evaluate the model at $\bx_{t_k}$ and convert its output into
        $\bx_0^\theta$ and $\beps_\theta$.
  \item Compute $\bx_{t_{k-1}}$ using the DDIM formula with $s=t_{k-1}$ and $t=t_k$.
\end{enumerate}
The number of sampling steps $K$ may be much smaller than the number of training timesteps, which is the
main practical advantage of DDIM-style generation.

One practical detail is clipping.  If $\bx_0^\theta$ is clipped to the valid image range, the predicted
noise should be recomputed from Equation~\eqref{eq:eps_from_x0}.  This keeps the pair
$(\bx_0^\theta,\beps_\theta)$ consistent with the observed $\bx_t$.

\section{Numerical Notes}

Several edge cases matter in code:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At $t=0$, $\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}=0$, so formulas that divide by this term need a small numerical
        guard or should avoid noise reconstruction at exactly zero.
  \item At very late times, $\alpha_t$ can be close to zero, so reconstructing $\bx_0^\theta$ from a
        noise prediction can amplify model error.
  \item The square-root argument in Equation~\eqref{eq:ddim_step} must be non-negative.  The standard
        DDIM choice $\sigma_{s,t}=\eta\Sigma_{s,t}$ with $0\leq\eta\leq1$ satisfies this for valid
        schedules.
  \item If the sampler skips many training indices, the neural network is asked to make larger reverse
        moves.  This is faster, but sample quality may depend more strongly on the schedule and model
        parameterization.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The key identity behind both DDPM and DDIM is the closed-form forward marginal
$\bx_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\bx_0+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\beps$.  DDPM uses the Gaussian posterior
$q(\bx_s|\bx_t,\bx_0)$ and substitutes a learned clean-sample estimate.  DDIM rearranges the same
quantities into a step involving $\bx_0^\theta$, $\beps_\theta$, and a tunable stochasticity parameter
$\eta$.  With $\eta=0$, sampling becomes deterministic and can use a small number of reverse steps;
with $\eta=1$, the variance matches the DDPM posterior variance.
